\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Desktop Robot}
We built a differential-drive desktop robot that runs ROS~2 Jazzy on a Raspberry Pi~5, driving two Dynamixel XL330 motors (Figure~\ref{fig:robot}). A four-microphone array (ReSpeaker Mic Array v2.0) provides always-on voice input and a plug-in speaker provides voice output; the robot carries no exteroceptive sensors, so language is its only channel to and from the user. We intentionally kept the robot this minimal to comply with our study design. The round embodiment is equally deliberate: it presents no visually salient front, leaving the robot's orientation ambiguous so that participants must establish and negotiate frames of reference through the interaction itself.

\subsection{Deployment and Setting}
We recruited 12 participants from our campus community and conducted a disclosed Wizard-of-Oz study, in which participants were informed of the nature of the study when signing the consent form. The robot was teleoperated by a researcher, who drove it with a wireless gamepad and voiced its spoken responses. Participants were asked to communicate directly with the robot using only low-level movement instructions. When they issued high-level instructions, such as \emph{``go toward me''} or \emph{``go toward the vessel,''} the robot did not carry them out, and participants had to rephrase using low-level movement primitives. Participants were encouraged to think aloud throughout, completed Task~1 followed by Task~2, and took part in a post-task interview. All sessions were video-recorded.

\subsubsection{Task 1: Exploration}
Task~1 recreated an everyday desktop scene: a laptop, a vessel, a cup, and other office items were scattered across the surface (Figure~\ref{fig:study1}). The robot started at the corner diagonally opposite the participant, who then used verbal instructions to bring the robot toward them while steering it around the obstacles along the way.

\subsubsection{Task 2: Projected Trajectory}
Task~2 asked participants to guide the robot along predefined trajectories drawn on the desktop, each ending at their position. Whereas the open-ended first task left room for improvisation, this task demanded more precise and concise wording to keep the robot on the line. To let participants familiarize themselves with the task, we ordered the three trajectories by ascending difficulty: a simple right-angle turn, a zig-zag line, and a complex route (Figure~\ref{fig:study2}).

\subsection{Video Analysis}
Three researchers independently watched the session videos and drafted coding schemes of the strategies participants used to interact with the robot, then met to consolidate them into a shared, final scheme. We built a browser-based video annotator (Figure~\ref{fig:annotator}) that segments each session into contiguous, labeled intervals, and each researcher independently coded the videos with it. The resulting annotations were exported as structured files of time-stamped, labeled segments for aggregation and analysis. Participants' think-aloud remarks and post-task interviews were also transcribed from the recordings and analyzed alongside the coded segments.
